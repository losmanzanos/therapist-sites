Deep Brain Reorienting in Fort Collins

DBR works with the first split second of a hard moment, the one your body remembers even when your mind has moved on. Quiet, slow, and you never have to tell the whole story.

Ask whether DBR is a fit

What DBR is

Deep Brain Reorienting is a newer trauma therapy developed by Dr. Frank Corrigan, a Scottish psychiatrist. It works at the level of the brainstem, where your body decides something is wrong before you have words for it.

Every overwhelming moment starts the same way. Your head and neck turn toward it. Tension gathers in your face. Then comes a jolt of shock, and only after that the flood of feeling. In most trauma therapy we work with the feeling. DBR goes back a step, to the turn and the shock, and lets your nervous system finish a reaction it never got to complete.

That matters because the shock is what turns the volume up on everything after it. Lower the shock and the grief, fear, or shame that follows tends to come down with it. Nothing about the memory has to be argued with. The body just stops bracing.

What a session looks like

DBR is quieter than most people expect. There’s no bilateral stimulation and very little talking. Most of the session is you noticing your own body with me close by.

  1. Landing

    We start by finding where you are in your body right now, before any memory comes in. This is the anchor we come back to.

  2. Choosing a target

    A moment, a trigger, or just a feeling you can’t shake. A sentence or two is enough. You don’t have to describe it.

  3. Following the sequence

    I guide your attention to the tension in your head, face, and neck, and we stay with it. The shock underneath it gets room to move through.

  4. Settling

    We finish back in your body in the present. Afterward you may feel tired, or lighter, or oddly calm. All of those are normal.

Who it tends to help

DBR was built for the trauma that happened early, or went on for years, or has no clear memory attached to it at all.

  • Childhood and attachment wounds from before you had words for what was happening.

  • Complex trauma where there isn’t one memory to point at, just a feeling that has always been there.

  • A startle reflex, a clench in your jaw or neck, or a body that braces around certain people.

  • People who have done a lot of talk therapy and understand their history, but still react the same way.

  • People for whom EMDR felt like too much, too fast.

DBR next to EMDR and parts work

I don’t pick one method and run everything through it. EMDR is excellent when there are specific memories to process. Parts work is where we go when different parts of you want different things. DBR fits underneath both. It reaches the reactions that formed before memory, and it can make the other work gentler because the body isn’t fighting it the whole way.

When dissociation is in the picture, DBR has an advantage: it doesn’t ask you to go back into the story. We stay with sensation in the present, in small doses, and I watch for the point where you start to drift. If that happens, we stop and land again.

The research is still young, a handful of studies including one randomized trial, and the early results for PTSD are encouraging. I’ll be straight with you about what it can and can’t do.

Things people worry about

“I don’t remember much. Will that be a problem?”

No. DBR doesn’t need the memory. It works from the body’s reaction, which is still there whether or not the story is.

“Is it going to bring everything up?”

It tends to do the opposite. Because we work at the point before the feeling floods in, most people find DBR quieter than they expected. You can stop at any moment.

“How is this different from EMDR?”

EMDR uses eye movements or tapping while you hold a memory in mind, and it moves through a memory start to finish. DBR has no bilateral stimulation and stays with the first physical reaction. Some people do both at different points. We’ll decide together which fits where you are.

“Does it work over video?”

Yes. DBR relies on your attention to your own body, which travels fine. I keep sessions a bit slower on telehealth and check in more often.

“How many sessions?”

It depends on how early and how layered the trauma is. Some targets shift in a few sessions. Deeper attachment wounds take longer, and we fold DBR into the rest of our work rather than treating it as a separate course.
