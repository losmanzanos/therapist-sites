Dissociation and parts work

For lost time, internal conflict, or feeling like more than one person. Grounded in Structural Dissociation theory, with real time spent on stabilization before anything else.

Talk it through on a free consult

What it can look like

Dissociation is common after trauma, and it shows up in more ways than most people expect. A lot of people don’t have a word for it until someone names it.

  • Losing time, or finding things you don’t remember doing.

  • Watching yourself from a step away, like a movie.

  • The world going flat, foggy, or far off.

  • Inner voices that argue, or one that never lets up.

  • Reacting like a much younger version of yourself.

  • Big gaps in what you remember from childhood.

Parts, and why they exist

One part of you goes to work, pays the bills, and keeps life moving. Other parts carry what happened.

In Structural Dissociation theory, these are the Part That Goes on With Everyday Life and the Parts That Carry What Happened. The second group may hold fear, anger, or grief. One may expect to be left. Another may get very small or very quiet. Some notice the slightest change in someone’s voice. Some carry sensations, images, or beliefs that don’t make sense from where you are today.

These parts aren’t defective. They learned from experience, and sometimes what they learned was essential for survival. The trouble is that they may still be responding to information from another time.

The walls were a solution

Sometimes a system survives by keeping experiences separate. One part handles daily life. Another holds the fear. Another stays on alert so nobody else has to. That separation can be remarkably adaptive. It’s the nervous system saying: you hold this, I’ll handle that, we can’t know all of it at once.

So we don’t treat the walls as proof that something is broken. We get curious about why they were needed. And we don’t tear them down all at once, because any pack would panic if every gate opened at the same time. We build communication instead.

Building communication

The part of you that lives in today knows things the other parts may not: you’re older now, you’re here, that person isn’t, you can leave, you can ask for help. Part of the work is carrying that information inward. Not by telling a frightened part to stop being scared, but by inviting it to look.

Communication runs the other way, too. A part may be signaling that it’s scared, or doesn’t know where you are, or thinks you’re still trapped. The everyday part doesn’t have to get overwhelmed in order to listen. It can stay rooted in today while turning toward what another part is saying.

Separation keeps resources apart, too. The scared part may not know what you know now, and the sensitivity, intuition, and survival instincts those parts hold can become yours to use, too.

How we work

The phases overlap and loop back. That’s normal.

  1. Stabilization

    Safety, regulation, and getting oriented to the present. Trauma-Informed Stabilization Treatment (TIST) helps your parts cooperate instead of compete.

  2. Processing

    When your system is ready, we work with the memories your parts are carrying, often with EMDR adapted for dissociation.

  3. Integration

    Life gets bigger. More of you has access to more of what you know, and your parts don’t have to work so hard.

You don’t need a diagnosis to start. I work with the full range of dissociation, including DID and OSDD, and we go at the pace your system can handle.
