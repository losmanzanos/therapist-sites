Relational trauma and abuse recovery

Trauma bonds, coercive control, and what’s left after you get out. For survivors of emotional, psychological, physical, or sexual abuse.

Request a free consult

What it leaves behind

Abuse inside a relationship changes how you see yourself, other people, and the world. It often keeps working long after the relationship is over.

  • Shame, guilt, or blaming yourself for what happened.

  • Trouble trusting anyone, including your own read on people.

  • Missing someone who hurt you, and hating that you do.

  • Anxiety, depression, or PTSD symptoms that came along with it.

  • Going back, or being afraid you will.

How we work on it

Healing from this takes more than time. It takes a careful approach that rebuilds safety, self-worth, and connection.

Understanding the pattern

We look at how early relationships shaped your responses, so the pull toward certain people starts to make sense and loses some of its grip. From there we work on boundaries, and on what a secure connection actually feels like from the inside.

Loosening the trauma bond

Trauma bonds are built on cycles of harm and relief, and they’re strong for a reason. We work with the guilt and fear that keep you tied, so you can make decisions from a clear head instead of a panicked one.

Getting yourself back

Abuse tends to split people into the version that survived it and the parts that paid for it. Using ego state work, we bring those pieces back into conversation, until self-compassion is something you can feel, not just something you know you should have.

I offer therapy specific to domestic violence, sexual assault and abuse, and childhood abuse. If substance use became part of how you coped, that’s welcome here too.

What coercive control looks like

It rarely starts with anything you’d call abuse.

Most people I see didn’t think of their relationship as abusive while they were in it. There was no single moment. There was a slow narrowing.

  • Needing to explain where you were, who you talked to, why it took that long.
  • Money you can’t see or don’t control, or an allowance you have to justify.
  • Friends and family who slowly became “a problem,” until there weren’t many left.
  • Being told what happened didn’t happen, until you stopped trusting your own memory.
  • Punishment that doesn’t look like punishment: days of silence, withheld affection, a mood you have to manage.
  • Rules that change, so you’re always slightly wrong.
  • Threats aimed at what you love most: kids, pets, your job, your standing with people who matter.

If you read that list and felt your stomach drop, you don’t need to decide what to call it before we talk.
