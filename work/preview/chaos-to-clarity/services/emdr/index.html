EMDR therapy in Fort Collins

EMDR helps painful memories lose their charge, so they stop running the present. You won’t have to retell every detail for it to work.

Ask whether EMDR is a fit

What EMDR is

EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing. It’s an evidence-based approach for healing from trauma and distressing life experiences.

Talk therapy works on what you think about a memory. EMDR works on how the memory is stored. During a session you focus on a specific memory or feeling while following bilateral stimulation: guided eye movements, tapping, or alternating tones. That process helps your brain unstick a painful memory and file it where it belongs, in the past.

You don’t have to relive the whole thing. Plenty of people do EMDR on memories they never describe out loud, and you decide how much gets said.

The eight phases

EMDR follows a set structure. The early phases can take a while, especially if there’s a lot of history, and that’s on purpose. Nothing gets processed until you have reliable ways to settle afterward.

  1. History

    We map out what you’re dealing with and choose targets together.

  2. Preparation

    You learn ways to calm your body and come back to the present. We don’t move on until they work for you.

  3. Assessment

    We pick a memory and notice the image, the belief, and the body sensation tied to it.

  4. Desensitization

    Bilateral stimulation while you hold the memory loosely in mind, until it loses its charge.

  5. Installation

    A truer belief about yourself gets strengthened in its place.

  6. Body scan

    We check for leftover tension and clear what’s still there.

  7. Closure

    Every session ends with you steady, whether or not the memory is finished.

  8. Reevaluation

    Next time, we look at what shifted and decide where to go.

Who it tends to help

EMDR is especially well suited to the aftermath of abuse, whether it happened once or went on for years.

  • Complex trauma from abuse or neglect that went on for a long time.

  • Childhood experiences that still decide how you react today.

  • Emotional abuse that rewrote what you believe about yourself.

  • Flashbacks, nightmares, or a body that won’t stand down.

  • Shame or self-blame that doesn’t budge, no matter what you know.

EMDR when there’s dissociation

If you lose time, feel far away from yourself, or notice parts of you reacting very differently, standard EMDR can move too fast. I adapt it. We spend more time on stabilization, work in smaller pieces, and make sure the parts of you that carry the hardest memories are on board before we go there.

Over time, EMDR can lower the charge on traumatic memories, replace shame and worthlessness with something kinder and more accurate, and interrupt patterns tied to what happened. What’s left is a memory you can think about without your body going back there.

More on dissociation and parts work. For a general overview of EMDR, the EMDR International Association is a good place to start.

Things people worry about

“Will I forget what happened?”

No. The memory stays. What changes is the charge on it. Most people describe it afterward as “it’s still there, it just doesn’t grab me anymore.”

“Is this hypnosis?”

No. You’re awake and in control the whole time, and you can stop at any point. You’ll also talk less than you expect. I check in briefly between sets and otherwise stay out of the way.

“I don’t have a clear memory. I just have a feeling.”

That’s workable. We can start from a body sensation, an image, or a belief about yourself and follow it from there. Plenty of EMDR targets never become a clear picture.

“What if I fall apart in session?”

This is why the preparation phase takes as long as it takes. Before we process anything you’ll have ways to settle that we’ve tested together, and every session ends with you steady, whether or not the memory is finished.

“Does it work over video?”

Yes. For telehealth I use on-screen bilateral stimulation or guide you through tapping. The research on remote EMDR is solid, and in practice it goes about the same.
