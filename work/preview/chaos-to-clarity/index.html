We all have a story.

And some chapters are harder to remember, or make sense of, than others.

Maybe you’ve told yours a dozen times and it hasn’t moved. Maybe you can’t tell it at all, because pieces are missing. Both are reasonable places to start.

This work goes past getting symptoms down. We figure out why your system learned to run the way it does, then build something that feels livable now. I’m practical and fairly direct, and I’m on your side.

Every pack has two leaders.

People used to picture wolf packs as a fight for rank, with one “alpha” on top. David Mech, the biologist who made that idea famous, spent years walking it back.

Wild wolves live in families. Two parents lead, and they split the work.

Facing out

One ranges

Travels, hunts, reads the terrain. Decides what’s actually a threat and what’s just wind in the trees.

Facing in

One keeps the den

Stays close to the pups. Settles them, feeds them, guards what’s inside, and knows where everyone is.

Trauma tends to take one of them offline.

Sometimes the scout never stops scanning. Every sound is danger, and nobody gets to rest. Sometimes no one is keeping the den, and the parts of you inside it scatter, go quiet, or turn on each other.

Our work brings both back. We start at the den with steadiness and safety, and by getting your parts on speaking terms. Then we look outward together and sort out what was dangerous then from what’s dangerous now.

Mech, L. D. (1999). Alpha status, dominance, and division of labor in wolf packs. Canadian Journal of Zoology, 77, 1196–1203.

Steady first. Then the hard stuff.

We don’t open the vault in week one. Survival asked you to adapt in ways that made sense at the time, and we figure out which of those adaptations you still need.

Stabilization comes first: safety in your body, a way back when you drift, and parts of you that cooperate instead of compete. If processing ever feels like being thrown back into it, we’re going too fast, and we slow down.

I’ll tell you what I’m noticing and why I’m suggesting something. You can always say no.

Questions people ask first

All questions
Do I have to talk about what happened in detail?

No. A lot of trauma work, EMDR included, doesn’t need a blow-by-blow account. We go at the speed your system can handle, and you decide what gets said out loud.

What if I’m not sure it’s “trauma”?

You don’t need a label to book a consult. If something keeps happening that you can’t explain or stop, that’s reason enough to talk.

Do you offer telehealth?

Yes, anywhere in Colorado, Iowa, or Arizona. In-person sessions are at my office on South Timberline Road in Fort Collins.

What happens on the free consult?

Fifteen minutes on the phone. You tell me a little about what’s going on, I tell you how I’d approach it, and we both get a feel for whether it’s a fit.

What’s your cancellation policy?

Cancel at least 24 hours ahead to avoid a $100 late-cancellation fee. More than 10 minutes late without a heads-up counts as a no-show.
