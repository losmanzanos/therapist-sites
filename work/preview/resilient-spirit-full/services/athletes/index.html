What it looks like

The part of the sport nobody films.

Most athletes who reach out are not struggling with their sport. They are struggling with what the sport has started to mean.

A bad game used to sting for a night. Now it follows you for a week, and the next game starts before you ever step on the field.

That is usually how it shows up. Sleep goes first the night before competition. The stomach goes next. You start keeping a running list of everything you did wrong, and it is always longer than the list of what went right, even after a win.

For some athletes it is food. Training gets harder, eating gets smaller, and the line between "fueling for performance" and a rule you are afraid to break gets blurry. For others it is a slump they cannot think their way out of, or an injury that took the sport away and left a question nobody prepared them for: who am I without this?

None of that means you are weak or that you want it less. It usually means you have been carrying it alone for a long time.

The goal is not to care less about your sport. It is to stop needing every result to prove you are enough.

How we work on it

Skills that hold up on game day.

Sessions are practical. You leave each one with something to try at the next practice, and we adjust based on what actually happened.

  1. 1

    Map the pressure

    Where the anxiety starts, what you tell yourself in the moment, and what you do to manage it: the rituals, the avoidance, the overtraining, the replaying.

  2. 2

    Change the conversation in your head

    Using CBT, we find the all-or-nothing scoreboard thinking and practice something that holds up under a bad half.

  3. 3

    Practice discomfort on purpose

    Pre-competition nerves don't need to disappear for you to play well. With ACT and exposure work, you get better at competing with them in the room.

  4. 4

    Build an identity that survives a bad season

    Values work for life outside the sport, so an injury, a cut, or graduation doesn't take everything with it.

Common reasons athletes reach out

If one of these is you, it's worth a call.

  • Anxiety before games, meets or matches
  • Replaying mistakes for days
  • Perfectionism and fear of failure
  • Fear of letting the team or a parent down
  • Slumps and lost confidence
  • Identity outside of sport
  • Injury, retirement or being cut
  • Restricting food or compulsive training
  • Body image in weight-class and aesthetic sports
  • Burnout and losing the love of the game

Eating concerns are common in athletes and easy to miss. If food or weight has become part of the pressure, read about how we treat eating disorders and body image.

Questions

Questions from athletes and parents.

Something else on your mind? Ask it on the free call, or text (843) 212-6965.

Is this sport psychology?

Not exactly. Sport psychology usually focuses on mental skills for performance. This is therapy: we work on the anxiety, perfectionism, eating concerns or identity questions underneath the performance. Most athletes find their performance improves as a side effect, but the focus is on you, not your stats.

My child is a high school athlete. Can I be involved?

Yes. For clients under 18, a parent or guardian is part of the intake, and we talk together about what is shared and what stays between your teen and their therapist. If your athlete won't come to therapy, SPACE lets you work on it as a parent.

I play in college out of state. Can we still meet?

Sessions happen by video, and you need to be physically in South Carolina or North Carolina at the time of each session. If you are in school elsewhere, we can meet during breaks at home, or help you find someone licensed where you are.

Do you work with performers who aren't athletes?

Yes. Musicians, dancers, students facing high-stakes testing, and professionals who freeze before a big presentation often deal with the same pressure.

The next game will come either way.

You can keep carrying this alone, or you can spend fifteen minutes finding out whether therapy would help.
