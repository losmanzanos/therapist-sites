Who we see  /  Eating disorder and body image therapy in South Carolina and North Carolina

It started as a plan. Now it runs the day.

Counting, weighing, skipping meals, bingeing, purging, or training to "earn" food. An eating disorder can look like discipline from the outside and feel like a rule you can't break on the inside. Recovery is possible, and you don't have to be at your lowest weight or your worst point to deserve help.

What it can look like

Eating disorders don't have one look.

Most people with an eating disorder are not visibly underweight. Plenty of them are high achievers whom nobody is worried about.

Food is rarely only about food. It is often the one thing that feels controllable when everything else doesn't.

That is why "just eat" or "just stop" never works. Restricting, bingeing or purging usually does something for you, like numbing anxiety, quieting the inner critic, or giving you a sense of control. Many of the behaviors you're struggling with once helped you get through something hard. If they were easy to stop, you would have stopped already.

Therapy starts there. We get curious about what the eating disorder is doing for you, build other ways to cope with what it was covering, and work toward eating that is regular, flexible and less loud in your head.

Body image gets real attention too. Not the poster version where you are supposed to love every inch of yourself, but a quieter goal: being able to live in your body without it running the show.

What we treat

From full diagnoses to the stuff that's "not that bad."

You don't need a diagnosis to reach out. If you are thinking about food and your body most of the day, that is enough.

  • Anorexia and restrictive eating
  • Bulimia
  • Binge eating
  • ARFID and food anxiety
  • Compulsive exercise
  • Disordered eating in athletes
  • Chronic dieting and food rules
  • Body checking and body image distress
  • Eating concerns alongside OCD or anxiety
  • Relapse after earlier treatment

A word on safety. Eating disorders can affect your heart and other organs even when you look and feel fine. We ask every client to see a physician during treatment, and we coordinate with your doctor and a dietitian when you have one. If you need more support than weekly outpatient therapy can offer, we will help you find the right level of care and stay involved where we can.

The rules

Rules you never agreed to.

Eating disorders run on rules that feel like your own idea. Tap one to cross it out.

How treatment works

A team around you, and a pace you can manage.

  1. An honest assessment

    What you are eating, what you are doing to compensate, how long it has been going on, and how your body is doing. No judgment, and no weighing you on camera.

  2. The right team

    We confirm you have a physician involved and talk about a dietitian. Outpatient eating disorder treatment works best when everyone is working from the same plan.

  3. Steady changes

    Using CBT, ACT and exposure work, we chip away at food rules, fear foods and the urge to compensate, one manageable step at a time.

  4. The rest of your life

    Perfectionism, anxiety, sport pressure and the inner critic usually sit right next to the eating disorder. We work on those too, so recovery holds.

Questions

Before you call.

Something else on your mind? Ask it on the free call, or text (843) 212-6965.

Is online therapy enough for an eating disorder?

For many people, yes, especially when a physician is monitoring physical health and a dietitian is involved. If your medical picture needs closer care, we will tell you honestly and help you find it.

Do you work with teens and their parents?

Yes. With teens, parents are usually part of treatment. You see what is happening at meals in a way nobody else does.

I am an athlete and my coach thinks I am just "dialed in." Is this for me?

If you are scared of foods, training to make up for eating, or losing your period or your energy, it is worth a conversation. See our page on therapy for athletes.

What if I am not ready to change yet?

That is common and it is fine to start there. Part of early treatment is talking honestly about what you would be giving up.

Recovery is possible.

You don't have to be sure you are ready. Fifteen minutes on the phone is a fine place to start.
