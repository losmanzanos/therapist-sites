Anxiety or Burnout? How to Tell the Difference

October 15, 2026 · 5 min read · Katie Fortunato, LPC

You're exhausted by two in the afternoon and wide awake at midnight. Unloading the dishwasher feels like a mountain. You snapped at your kid over a missing shoe. And underneath all of it, a question keeps nagging: is this anxiety or burnout, and does it even matter which?

It matters a little. Here's the short answer. Burnout is mostly depletion, and it usually traces back to a specific load you've carried too long. Anxiety is mostly alarm, and it tends to come along wherever you go, including the places that should feel restful. Most people who call us trying to sort this out have some of both. Knowing which one is louder tells you what to change first.

What burnout feels like from the inside

Burnout has a source. You can usually point at it.

The job where you've covered two open positions since spring. The parent with dementia who calls six times a day. The toddler who hasn't slept through the night in a year. The deployment where you've been the only adult in the house for months.

It builds slowly. At first you just push harder. Then the things you used to care about go flat. A teacher who loved her classroom in August is dreading Sunday nights by October, counting down to Thanksgiving break and feeling guilty about it. A nurse stops asking her patients the extra question, because she has nothing left to hold the answer.

Cynicism is a big tell. So is a kind of numbness where you go through the motions and feel very little.

The hopeful part: when the load actually lifts, people tend to come back. A real week off, phone in a drawer, and you start to feel like yourself again. The trouble is that for most people the load never lifts.

Anxiety follows you on vacation

Anxiety works differently. Instead of running out, your system runs hot.

You finally get to the cabin in Woodland Park. The kids are asleep, the fire's going, and your brain starts in. Did I answer that email. Is that mole new. What if the furnace goes out at home while we're gone. Your shoulders stay up around your ears. You lie awake rehearsing a conversation that may never happen.

Anxiety also likes to move. Solve one worry and another walks right in and takes its seat. That's useful information: if the content keeps changing but the feeling stays the same, the content probably isn't the problem.

In the body, anxiety tends to show up as a racing heart, a tight chest, a stomach that won't settle, restless legs at bedtime. Burnout feels heavy. Anxiety feels wired.

Why so many people have both

Here's where it gets tangled. The two feed each other, and they share a lot of symptoms: poor sleep, irritability, trouble focusing, headaches, pulling away from friends.

Anxious people tend to over-function. They take the extra project because saying no feels risky. They double-check everything, plan for every possibility, and carry the mental load for the whole household. Do that for a few years and you burn out.

Then burnout drains the reserves you'd normally use to keep anxiety in check. You're tired, so the worries get louder. You have less patience, so small problems feel huge. Now you're anxious about being burned out, and burned out from being anxious.

I see this loop constantly in Colorado Springs. Military spouses, ICU nurses, teachers, small business owners, parents keeping a household afloat on one income. From the outside they look fine. They are very, very good at looking fine.

This is also why rest alone doesn't always work. If it's mostly burnout, rest helps. If anxiety is what's driving the overwork, you'll rest for a weekend and go right back to taking on too much, because the reason you took it on hasn't changed. In anxiety therapy in Colorado Springs, we usually look at both layers: what has to change in your week right now, and what your nervous system learned a long time ago that keeps you braced. When older experiences are keeping the alarm stuck on, EMDR can be part of that work. If you're curious what a first appointment is like, here's what to expect in your first therapy session.

Anxiety or burnout? A quick check

A blog post can't diagnose you, and I wouldn't try. These questions can point you in a direction, though:

  • If the thing draining you disappeared tomorrow, would you feel relief, or would your mind go looking for the next worry?
  • Do you feel better after a real day off, or does the worry pack itself in your bag?
  • Is the main feeling empty, or on edge?
  • Can you name the source, or is it more of a general hum?
  • Did this start with a specific change (a new job, a new baby, a PCS move), or have you felt some version of it most of your life?
  • When you picture saying no to something, what shows up first: relief or dread?

Relief, a clear source, a recent start? Burnout is probably the bigger piece. Worry that travels, a lifelong hum, dread at the thought of saying no? Anxiety is likely doing more of the work. Mixed answers are normal. Most people's are.

Here's one thing to try this week. Pick a single recurring commitment that costs you more than it gives back (the committee you joined because nobody else would, the Thursday call you dread, the extra shift you always say yes to) and pause it for one week. Don't fill the gap with something else. Then pay attention to what happens in your body when that hour comes around. If you mostly feel lighter, that's information about burnout. If a wave of guilt or worry shows up and won't leave, that's information about anxiety. Either way, by Friday you'll know something about yourself that you don't know today.
