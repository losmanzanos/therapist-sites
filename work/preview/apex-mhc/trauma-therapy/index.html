The Work

Something that ended is still happening.

Therapy for trauma and PTSD in adults, with EMDR when you want it. At a pace you set, without having to tell the whole story first. Telehealth across Colorado.

Most people who come in for trauma do not call it that. They describe a startle that is out of all proportion. Sleep that breaks at the same hour every night. A stretch of highway they drive around, a smell in a hospital corridor, a tone of voice that takes the rest of the afternoon with it.

They know it is over. The trouble is that part of them is still keeping watch.

Something that happened once got filed as something that is still happening, and the part of you that did the filing was doing its job at the time. Therapy for trauma is mostly the slow work of getting that file corrected.

What it looks like a few years on

  • Sitting where you can see the door, and noticing you always do
  • Sleep that stopped working, or that only works with a drink first
  • A body that braces at sounds other people do not hear
  • Whole parts of your own history you route around
  • Flatness where feeling used to be, or anger with nowhere better to go

Some of it arrives as a memory. More of it arrives as a reaction with no memory attached, which is harder to explain to anyone and easier to blame on yourself.

Who brings it in

People who lived through one very bad day, and people who lived through years of smaller ones. First responders and medical staff, who see more in a shift than most people see in a decade and are expected to be fine by the next one. Adult children of difficult families. Veterans and service members, and survivors of domestic violence, each of whom has a page of their own here because their version has its own shape.

You do not need a diagnosis to start, and you do not need to be sure that what happened was “bad enough.” If you are asking that question, it usually was.

Where we start

With this week. What it is costing you now, in your body and in your day. For a lot of people that means sleep and startle first, because everything else is harder to work on while you are running on five hours.

You will not be asked for the full story up front. I ask for what I need when it is useful, and “not yet” is always an acceptable answer. You set the pace, and you can change it any week.

On EMDR

EMDR is a structured way of reprocessing one specific memory at a time. It is not hypnosis and it is not an hour of talking about your childhood. When a memory still behaves like a present event, arriving whole and with the feeling attached, it is one of the better-evidenced approaches there is.

It is also optional. It works badly on someone who has been talked into it, so nobody gets talked into it here.

When it is not a memory problem

Not everything that follows trauma is a memory waiting to be processed. When the feeling arrives at full volume before you get a say, dialectical skills come first. When a thought like “it was my fault” has been running unexamined for years, cognitive work takes it apart. Some of what people carry is moral injury rather than fear: having done, or seen, or failed to stop something that does not sit right. That needs a different approach than fear does, and it gets one.

Underneath all of it is attachment work, which is the plain business of noticing that you learned to expect a particular thing from people and are still expecting it from people who are not those people.

Online, and whether that is enough

For most adults the research says yes, and that includes trauma work and EMDR. What you need is a private place to sit and a connection that holds. Some people find it easier to do this work from their own chair than from a stranger’s couch.

It is not the right setting for an acute crisis, or for someone who needs same-day, in-person care. If that is where you are, I will tell you on the first call and help you find the right level of care. I also do not write evaluations for courts or disability claims.

If you are in immediate danger or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988, or call 911. The Colorado Crisis Line is 1-844-493-8255, or text TALK to 38255.

Questions Asked before a first call

What people ask about trauma work.

Do I have to talk about what happened?
Not in detail, and not before you are ready. Some of the most useful trauma work happens with very little of the story told out loud. I ask for what I need when it is useful, and “not yet” is always an acceptable answer.
Can EMDR be done online?
Yes. The research on telehealth covers trauma work and EMDR, and for most adults it holds up. You need a private place to sit and a steady connection. EMDR is always optional, and plenty of people do this work without it.
Do I need a PTSD diagnosis to come in?
No. Plenty of people who come in for trauma have never had one. If you use insurance, a diagnosis is part of how sessions get paid for, and the fees page explains what that means for your record.
It happened a long time ago. Is it too late?
No. A memory that still behaves like a present event does not expire. People start this work years and sometimes decades after the fact, and the timing has more to do with when it became possible than with when it happened.
