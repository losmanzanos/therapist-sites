When Someone You Love Is in a Psychiatric Hospital: What Happens Next

The ER called. Or the police did. Or your person called you from a unit phone and you could hear the hallway behind them. Whatever got you here, here is what tends to happen next and what to do about it.

The first 24 hours

You may not be allowed to talk to anyone. Privacy law means the unit can't confirm your person is there unless they've signed a release, and a lot of people don't sign anything in the first day. This feels like being shut out. It's usually paperwork, not a judgment about you.

What you can do: call the unit, say who you are, and say you have information that may help the treatment team. They can listen even if they can't talk. Then ask your person, when you reach them, to sign a release of information with your name on it. That one form changes everything that follows.

Write down the name of the unit, the social worker or discharge planner, and the main number. You'll need all three more than once.

What the hold actually is

In Colorado, an involuntary admission usually starts as a 72-hour hold. You'll hear it called an M-1 hold, after the form the hospital files. That's the legal window for evaluation. It doesn't mean your person will be there exactly three days; they can be released sooner if they're judged safe, or the hospital can ask a court for more time if they're not. After the hold, many people stay voluntarily.

Ask the social worker two things: what the treatment team thinks needs to be different before discharge, and what the earliest realistic discharge day is. You will rarely get a firm answer. Ask anyway, every day, politely. It keeps you on the list of people they update.

The discharge meeting

At some point you'll be invited to a discharge planning meeting, sometimes with a day's notice, sometimes with an hour's. Go, by phone if you have to. This is the single most important half hour of the whole stay.

Bring these questions:

  • What level of care is being recommended next, and has it been set up or just suggested? (What the levels mean.)
  • Is there a first appointment on the calendar, with a date and a name? "They'll call you" is not an appointment.
  • What medications are they leaving with, how many days' worth, and who prescribes the refill?
  • What's the safety plan, and is there a copy I can have?
  • Who do we call if things fall apart on Saturday night?

If the answers are vague, say so in the meeting. A discharge plan with no appointment attached is the most common way people end up right back in the ER.

The first week home

Expect your person to be exhausted, flattened by new medication, embarrassed, or all three. Expect yourself to be hypervigilant. Both are normal and both are temporary.

Keep the first week boring on purpose. Fewer visitors, fewer big conversations, regular meals and sleep. Don't make them process the hospital stay with you unless they bring it up. Do make sure the first follow-up appointment happens, even if you have to drive.

Decide, before they get home, what you will and won't do. Will you hold the medication? Will they stay with you or go back to their own place? Who are the people they can't bring over? Say these things once, plainly, and early. Boundaries announced in the middle of a fight don't hold.

What this does to you

Families run on adrenaline through the admission and then collapse the week after. Guilt shows up ("I should have seen it"), anger shows up ("how could they do this to us"), and relief shows up and makes the guilt worse. None of that makes you a bad parent, partner, or sibling. It makes you a person who just went through something.

You're allowed to need support that is just for you. That can be a friend who's been through it, a family group, or a consultation where you get to ask the questions nobody at the hospital had time for.
