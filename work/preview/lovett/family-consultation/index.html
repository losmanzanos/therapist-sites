Family consultation · Denver and online

Someone you love is in the system. Nobody is explaining it to you.

A psychiatric hold. A treatment program. A jail cell, or a couch after release. A diagnosis that came with a pamphlet and no one to call. I spent sixteen years working inside those systems. I can tell you what the words mean, what usually happens next, and how to help without disappearing into it.

Who this is for

The people around the person.

Parents whose adult son was just admitted and who don't know if they're allowed to call the unit. A sister trying to figure out whether residential or IOP is the better recommendation to push for. A wife whose husband comes home from prison in six weeks. Grandparents raising the kids while their daughter is in treatment for the third time.

You are not the patient. You are the person holding everything together, and the hospital, the program, and the court are all built to talk to the patient, not to you.

Sometimes what you need is emotional support. Often what you need first is information and a plan.

The questions families bring

Here's what this means. Here's what to consider. Here's a next step.

Most of what I hear in a first consultation sounds like this:

  • What does this diagnosis actually mean for day to day life?
  • What's the difference between inpatient, residential, PHP, and IOP, and which one should we be asking for?
  • What happens after discharge, or after release? Who is responsible for what?
  • How do we support him without paying for the thing that's hurting him?
  • What boundary can we actually hold, and what happens when we hold it?
  • How do we talk to the kids, or to our parents, about what's going on?
  • Medicaid, the public defender, the case manager, the probation officer. Who do we even call?
  • How do we deal with our own fear, anger, guilt, and exhaustion while all of this is happening?

You don't need to know the vocabulary before you come. That's my job.

What a consultation looks like

One conversation, or a few. Structured, not clinical.

We start with the situation as it stands today. I ask a lot of practical questions: where your person is, what they've been told, what paperwork exists, who the players are. Then I explain what I'm seeing, in plain language, including the parts the system isn't going to say out loud.

From there we build a plan you can hold. What to ask the treatment team this week. What to put in writing. What a reasonable boundary sounds like coming out of your mouth. Where the resources are and which ones are worth your time.

Some families need one session and a follow-up call. Some want ongoing support through a long stretch of illness or a long sentence. Some decide, partway through, that what they really want is family therapy, and that's available too. Nothing gets decided for you.

Sessions are in person in Denver or by secure video anywhere in Colorado. Reach out and I'll walk you through the format and the fee before anything is booked.

What this is not

Not an emergency line. Not a lecture. Not a reason for shame.

If someone is in immediate danger, call or text 988 or call 911. I'm not on call, and I won't pretend to be. The time to reach out is usually right after the immediate crisis passes, when the questions start and the system goes quiet.

I also won't moralize about addiction, mental illness, or a record. I spent years working with people who had all three, and with the families who loved them anyway. The complicated parts of your life don't disqualify you from getting clear, kind help with them.

And I won't tell you what to do with your own family. I'll tell you what I've seen work, what tends to backfire, and what the trade-offs are. The decisions stay yours.

Background

Sixteen years inside the systems you're trying to deal with.

Most of my career has been in behavioral health with people living with serious mental illness, substance use disorders, chronic homelessness, and involvement with the criminal justice system. Direct clinical care, community mental health, program development, clinical supervision, and executive leadership.

Most recently I helped build and run a behavioral health program inside a nonprofit serving people returning to the community after incarceration: standing up clinical services, supporting Medicaid implementation, supervising clinical teams, and training staff. I know how a discharge plan gets written, how a case manager's caseload actually works, and which phone calls get returned.

I'm a Licensed Professional Counselor (MA, LPC) and a Certified Clinical Trauma Professional (CCTP) in Colorado. More about my background.
