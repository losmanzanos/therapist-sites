Therapy After Narcissistic Abuse

For the stretch after a relationship that taught you to doubt your own memory. With Angelique Danza, LMFT, in Temecula or over secure video.

What It Leaves Behind

The doubt outlasts the relationship

People expect to feel relief when it ends. A lot of them feel confused instead. They replay conversations to check whether it was really that bad. They draft the text, delete it, and wonder if they were the difficult one all along.

If your experiences were regularly minimized, denied, or turned around until you were the problem, that doubt isn’t a character flaw. It’s what months or years of that will do to anyone.

From the outside the relationship may have looked healthy, even enviable. From the inside it tends to look more like this:

  • Being told that what you remember didn’t happen that way, or didn’t happen at all
  • Affection handed out as a reward and pulled back as a punishment
  • Steady criticism that arrives as “just being honest”
  • Friends and family drifting out of your life so gradually you barely noticed
  • The pedestal, then the fall from it, then the pedestal again
  • A standing sense that you are never quite enough

It doesn’t have to have been a partner. A parent can do this. So can a sibling, a boss, or a friend of twenty years.

In Session

We start with what you remember

I’m certified in narcissistic abuse treatment, and this is one of the things I see people for most in individual therapy.

We don’t spend the hour relitigating every argument or building a case against the other person. We work on getting your footing back: getting clear about what happened, trusting your own read on things again, and learning to tell a safe relationship from one that only feels familiar.

Some of that is Cognitive Behavioral Therapy. Beliefs like “I overreact” or “I’m impossible to love” were installed by repetition, and they can be examined one at a time. Some of it is slower. There is often real grief for the relationship you thought you were in, and that part mostly needs room.

You set the pace. Nobody here will tell you what to call it, or what to do about the person.

Common Questions

What people ask first

Do I need to be sure it was narcissistic abuse before I call?

+

No. Most people aren’t sure, and the doubt is part of what the relationship left behind. You don’t need the right word for it. Describe what happened and we’ll start from there.

Can I start therapy if I’m still in the relationship?

+

Yes. Some people come in after it has ended, some while they are deciding, and some with no plan to leave. Therapy doesn’t require a decision first.

What if the person was a parent, not a partner?

+

Then that’s the relationship we work with. The same patterns show up with a parent, a sibling, a boss or a close friend, and growing up inside them leaves its own kind of doubt.

Will you diagnose my ex?

+

No. I can’t diagnose someone I haven’t met, and you don’t need a diagnosis for them in order to recover. The work is about what you lived through and what it did to you.
