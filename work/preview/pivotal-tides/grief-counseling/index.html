What Counts as Grief

Bigger than most people think

Grief is one of the three areas I focus on most. People come to see me after a parent dies, or a husband, or a friend.

They also come after a divorce they asked for. After a friendship that slowly disappeared, a child moving out, a diagnosis, a career they had to give up, years of infertility. You can grieve a home, your health, a sense of safety, or the future you were sure you were going to have.

Some losses never get a clear ending. A person can still be in your life and no longer be reachable. That kind of ambiguous loss is hard to name, and harder to explain to people who expect you to be over it by now.

All of it counts.

“Grief is not a race, and healing is not measured by how quickly we stop missing what we lost.”
Angelique Danza, in the Journal
In Session

There’s no schedule you’re behind on

The five stages of grief give some people useful words. They were never meant as a route you have to follow in order. Anger can show up after acceptance. Sadness and relief can land in the same afternoon. That isn’t going backward.

Grief can also come late. Plenty of people hold it together through the arrangements, the paperwork, the kids and the job, then feel the full weight months later when everything goes quiet.

In our sessions there’s room for whatever is there, including guilt, numbness, irritability, and the exhaustion nobody warned you about. I use Cognitive Behavioral Therapy where it helps, often with the thoughts that keep circling at 2 a.m. Client-Centered Therapy covers the parts that simply need to be heard.

Later, sometimes much later, a different question can open up. David Kessler called it meaning: what do I do with what happened to me? Nobody has to rush toward it.

Common Questions

Asked by people who are grieving

Is it too late to get help with a loss from years ago?

+

No. Grief doesn’t keep a schedule. Some people feel it right away, some feel it months later once life goes quiet, and some feel waves of it years on. Any of those is a reasonable time to come in.

Can I come in for a loss that wasn’t a death?

+

Yes. Divorce, infertility, an estranged family member, a career or a version of yourself you had to let go of: these are real losses and they belong in grief counseling.

Do you take insurance for grief counseling?

+

Aetna, Anthem Blue Cross, Carelon and Cigna/Evernorth are accepted, along with out-of-network benefits. Sessions are $175 if you pay directly. Coverage depends on your plan, and I’m happy to verify it before your first session.

Can we meet by video?

+

Yes. Sessions can be at the Temecula office or over secure, HIPAA-compliant video. Some people find it easier to talk about a loss from their own home.
